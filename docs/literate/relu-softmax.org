A neural network's layers are array operations: multiply by weights,
add a bias, then a function applied to every value. Two of those
functions turn up everywhere. ReLU keeps positive values and makes every
negative one 0. Softmax turns a row of scores into shares that add up
to 1 -- how sure the network is of each answer. This document shows a
30-second video of both, made from a real network's arrays, and how
every part of the video comes from X_eTaL.

* Purpose: what the video shows, and why

The network is X_eTaL-gpu's tiny-net: four examples of three inputs,
a dense layer of five with ReLU, a dense layer of two with softmax.
X_eTaL-gpu compiles the same X_eTaL functions -- its Accel library -- to
run on Apple and NVIDIA GPUs and checks the GPU's answers against the
X_eTaL evaluator. Here the evaluator runs them, and the arrays it gives
are drawn:

- 0 to 11 s, ReLU. The first layer's output z1 (4 by 5) stands as bars
  on a glass floor: positive values orange and up, negative ones blue
  and hanging below. The negatives turn red and shrink to 0, leaving
  their outlines; what is left is h, ReLU's output.
- 11.5 to 30 s, softmax. The second layer's output z2 (4 by 2), a row
  of two scores for each example, stands as four groups of two bars.
  Softmax's four steps happen to all of them at once: subtract each
  row's largest, raise e to each value, add up each row (a white bar),
  divide by the sum. Each row ends as one column of its two shares,
  every column 1 tall.

The point for a reader is the shape of the computation -- which values
change, which do not, and why softmax's results add up to 1 -- told by
the arrays themselves rather than by a listing.

* The chain: X_eTaL, Python, Blender, video

Nothing in the chain is written by hand for this video:

1. *X_eTaL works out the arrays.* The network's layers are X_eTaL-gpu's
   Accel library (~ac:d_ense~, ~ac:r_elu~, ~ac:s_oftmax~, ~ac:r_owMax~,
   ~ac:r_owSums~), pinned at a known commit (~GPU_COMMIT~).
2. *X_eTaL works out the video.* ~extensions/blender/demos/ReluSoftmax.xtl~
   turns those arrays into more arrays: every bar's place and size at
   every key, every color, the seconds each thing is shown, the camera's
   path, the captions' words. This is the only part made for this video.
3. *X_eTaL writes the Python.* The blender extension's ~Video.xtl~ (alias
   ~vd:~) turns each table into one line of Python: a call such as
   ~moves('z1', times, rows)~ with every number written out. Its
   player, the Python those calls run, is fixed text in the library, the
   same for every video, tested on its own; it decides nothing.
4. *Blender runs it.* The blender extension's ~bl:r_un!~ runs Blender in
   the background on the script; the player makes the objects and keys,
   and Blender's EEVEE renders 901 frames as an MP4.

The same arrays always give the same script, byte for byte.

* 3. Animation, at a high level

A video here is a timeline of groups of objects. Every object is a box
or a word; a group is one kind of thing -- z1's twenty bars, their
twenty labels, the outlines of the nine removed values. For each group
the program gives tables, one row an object:

- what it is at the start: a box's middle and size and color, a word's
  place, height and color;
- when it is shown: a span, from one second to another;
- its keys: at each of a few times, its place and size; between keys
  Blender eases from one to the next (Bezier), so a bar shrinks
  smoothly;
- its tints: at each of a few times, its color;
- and, for the whole video, the camera's keys -- where it is and what it
  looks at -- and the captions, each with its span.

That is all an animation is here: no physics, no simulation, nothing
Blender decides. Everything moves because a table says where it is at
which second.

The timing is an array too. ReLU runs from 0 to 11 s; softmax's steps
start at these seconds, and the video ends at 30:

A score's bar has seven keys: the score, held until the shift, the
shifted score, held until the e's, the e, held until the division, the
share. Holding a value (two keys with the same value a second apart)
is what makes each step a pause, then a move. Each row's sum is a
white bar beside its two, shown from the sum's step to just after the
division. The result is a column per row: its two shares stacked, the

* 8. The player: what Blender runs

The player is the Python at the top of every script, ~vd:head~: fixed
text in the library. Each call reads its numbers and does exactly what
they say. ~moves~ sets each object's place and size and keys them at
each time's frame; ~tints~ keys a material's color; ~shown~ keys
~hide_render~ off at a span's start and on after its end; ~camera~ keys
the camera and the point it looks at:
